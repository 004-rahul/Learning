### Settled 25 August 2026 - the internal transfer route is closed
The current employer does not offer overseas transfer or relocation. That is now a known fact, not an open question, and it closes several things at once: the L-1 route in Part 2, the "you are already L-1 eligible" note in Part 2, and the Part 8 checklist row that asks HR about India-to-US mobility precedent. None of those need chasing. Treat this addendum as superseding them.
This matters more than it first looks, because the research rated an internal transfer as the highest-conversion move available - 25 to 40 per cent, against under 2 per cent for cold US applications. Losing it removes the best route and the only clean path into the United States in one go.
What survives is the strategy, not the employer. Route 1 in Part 2 - join an India office, move later - still works, but only through a company that actually moves people, which is exactly what Part 3M lists. So Part 3M changes from a fallback into a live parallel track: a role at an India-based employer with proven mobility is worth taking on its own terms, because it restores the transfer route that the current job cannot provide.
Immediate consequence for the application list: with no transfer available and EB-2 closed to a three-year degree, the United States has no route worth spending hours on. Direct applications to Germany, the Netherlands and Poland, plus Part 3M employers in India, are where the hours go until the three verifications above come back.

#### Verification attempt, 29 August 2026 - what could and could not be checked
Ten web requests were spent trying to close the four Part 0A verifications. The outcome is worth recording, because it changes how these gaps can ever be closed at all.
•  Five official sources refused automated access outright, returning HTTP 403: canada.ca on two separate pages, ind.nl, make-it-in-germany.de and bmi.bund.de. That independently reproduces what the August research reported about canada.ca, so it is not a one-off. The practical consequence: these verifications cannot be completed by any research tool. They have to be done by you, in a browser, as a person. Do not commission more research expecting a different result - it will fail the same way.
•  Germany - the no-degree IT route does appear to exist, which is the good news. But two details surfaced that the August research did not record: German-language proficiency appears to be required as a rule on that route, and it may be a separate residence permit rather than the EU Blue Card itself, carrying a higher salary floor than the EUR 45,934.20 quoted above. This does not close the question, it sharpens it - the thing to establish is which of the two routes you actually qualify for, because they are not the same permit and do not have the same threshold.

## PART 1 - Behavioural mastery and the STAR story bank

### The follow-up probes (this is where interviews are actually won or lost)
Your first answer gets you maybe 40% of the score. The follow-ups get the rest. Pre-write answers to these for every story.
•  What was the data behind that decision?
•  What alternatives did you consider, and why did you reject them?
•  What was your specific contribution, as opposed to the team's?
•  What was the hardest part, technically?
•  Who disagreed with you, and how did you handle it?
•  What went wrong that you did not anticipate?
•  How did you measure success? What were the numbers before and after?
•  What would you do differently now?
•  What did you learn that you applied later?
•  How did this affect the customer or the business?
•  How long did it take, and did that match your estimate?
•  What happened after you left it - did it hold up?
The Bar Raiser will keep going until you reach the edge of your knowledge. That is the design of the round, not a sign it is going badly. When you reach the edge, say so: "I do not know the exact figure - what I remember is the order of magnitude, and here is how I would confirm it." That scores well. Inventing a number does not, and it is often detectable.

### Why the US is ranked last despite paying the most
CONFIRMED and hardened, 25 August 2026 - see Part 0A. Three additions. Cold applications from India convert at under 2 per cent, so the practical advice is not "rank it last" but "do not spend hours on it at all". EB-2 is structurally closed to a three-year BCA, because it requires a single-source four-year degree. And the L-1 escape hatch this section relies on is not available - the current employer does not offer transfer or relocation - so the United States should be dropped as an active channel rather than merely ranked last.
This is the conclusion most Indian engineers resist, so here it is plainly. On an H-1B or L-1 you are working in the United States on a status that belongs to your employer. Changing jobs is a legal event. Being laid off starts a countdown measured in weeks. Your spouse may not be able to work. And because employment-based green cards are capped per country of birth, the queue for an Indian national in the main professional categories is long enough that people plan their children's citizenship around it rather than their own.
None of that makes the US a bad idea. It makes it a specific idea: go for high earnings and a strong resume over a defined number of years, with your eyes open, ideally on an L-1A management track that opens EB-1C. Do not go expecting to settle.

## PART 4 - How to verify a company before you apply
Every company says it supports mobility. Most do not, in practice. And every job board is full of overseas postings from companies that cannot legally sponsor you. Twenty minutes of checking per company saves you evenings of wasted applications, and it is the difference between a pipeline and a lottery ticket.
<a id="s14-check-1-the-government-sponsor-register-do-this-first"></a>

### Check 1 - the government sponsor register (do this first)
Several countries publish a list of employers legally permitted to sponsor a foreign worker. If a company is not on the list, the conversation is over before it starts - no amount of enthusiasm from a hiring manager can fix it. This is the highest-value five minutes in your entire job search and almost nobody does it.

|---|---|---|

| Netherlands | Public register of recognised sponsors. A highly skilled migrant permit is legally impossible without one | ind.nl - "Public Register Recognised Sponsors" |

<a id="s14-check-2-the-linkedin-test-do-this-before-every-single-applic"></a>

### Check 2 - the LinkedIn test (do this before every single application)
•  Search the company name plus "Software Engineer", and filter the location to Toronto, Auckland, Sydney, Dublin, Amsterdam, Berlin, Copenhagen, London or Krakow.
•  Open ten profiles. Look at their career history, not their current title.
•  Did any of them start at that company in India and later appear in the foreign office? Or did any of them move to that country from India at all?
•  If several did, relocation is real. If none did, the mobility programme is marketing.
•  Bonus signal: how many engineers in that office have Indian names and non-local university degrees? A team that already contains migrants is a team whose manager has done the paperwork before and is not afraid of it.
This single check is worth more than any recruiter's assurance. Do it for every company on your shortlist, and record the answer in the application tracker in Document 11 so you never repeat the work.
<a id="s14-check-3-questions-to-ask-the-recruiter-ask-early-not-after-t"></a>

### Check 3 - questions to ask the recruiter (ask early, not after the offer)
<a id="s14-for-an-overseas-role-you-are-applying-to-directly"></a>

#### For an overseas role you are applying to directly
•  Is this role open to candidates who require visa sponsorship? - Ask this in the first message. It costs you nothing and saves you weeks.
•  Does the company hold a sponsor licence, and have you sponsored engineers from outside the country recently?
•  Who covers the visa costs, the relocation, and the dependant applications?
•  What is the realistic start date, allowing for visa processing?
•  Is the role permanent and local-contract, or a fixed-term deputation?
<a id="s14-for-an-india-role-you-intend-to-transfer-out-of"></a>

#### For an India role you intend to transfer out of
•  How many engineers moved from the India office to an overseas office in the last two years?
•  Is this role part of a global team, or does it sit in an India-only cost centre? This is the question that catches the trap.
•  Is there a formal internal mobility programme, and what is the eligibility period?
•  Does the company sponsor work visas for transferring employees, and who bears the cost?
•  Are there open roles in the overseas offices that an internal candidate could apply to today?
Many Global Capability Centres in India are structured as cost centres that deliver work for teams abroad but never move people into them. The work looks global; the career is not. That second question is how you tell the difference before you sign.
<a id="s14-red-flags"></a>

### Red flags
•  Nobody at the company has ever moved from India to an overseas office.
•  The recruiter answers "we support it case by case" and cannot name a single case.
•  The company is not on the sponsor register for a country that publishes one.
•  The India entity is a separate legal subsidiary with no corporate relationship to the foreign entity. The L-1 route legally requires a qualifying relationship.
•  The role is explicitly staffed to support a foreign team rather than to be part of one.
•  The overseas job posting says "must have existing right to work" - believe it, and move on.
•  A recruiter asks you to pay anything at all, for a visa, a placement, or a "processing fee". This is always a scam. Legitimate employers pay sponsorship costs, never the candidate.

The comparison that actually matters. A Rs 30 LPA India offer, a EUR 75,000 Dublin offer and a USD 180,000 Seattle offer are not three points on one scale. ### Referrals - an hour a week, higher return than an hour of LeetCode
•  Search LinkedIn for engineers at your target companies who share something with you: C#/.NET, Gujarat, VNSGU, a previous employer, or simply an Indian name in a Toronto or Dublin office.
•  Someone who moved from India to that exact office two years ago is the single most useful contact you can find. They have done the paperwork, they remember how hard it was, and they reply at a much higher rate than a random employee.
•  The message is short and specific: who you are, one line on the eDiscovery and .NET fit, the exact role ID, and a direct ask. Never a wall of text, never "can I pick your brain".
•  Ask about the move, not just the referral. "Did you relocate from India to the Dublin office? I am trying to understand whether that path is real at your company" gets replies from people who would ignore a referral request.
•  Track every message. Expect a low reply rate and do not take it personally - ten messages a week for a month is forty contacts and that produces referrals.
<a id="s14-when-they-say-we-cannot-sponsor"></a>

### When they say "we cannot sponsor"
You will hear this often. Two useful responses, and one thing never to do.
•  Ask whether the company has any office that can sponsor - a Dublin, Toronto, Krakow or Vancouver team sometimes can when the London or Seattle team cannot.
•  Ask to be kept on file for roles that are open to sponsorship, and connect with the recruiter anyway. Sponsor licences get applied for; policies change quarterly.
•  Never offer to work as a contractor, through a third party, or remotely from India for a local salary in the hope it converts later. It almost never converts, and it removes your leverage entirely.
<a id="s14-part-7-interview-questions-and-answers"></a>

## PART 7 - Interview questions and answers
These are the questions asked in almost every screening call and HR round, plus the ones you only get asked when you are applying from India to a job abroad. Part 1 of this document covers the deep behavioural and Leadership Principle stories. This part covers the conversational layer - the questions that decide whether you reach the technical rounds at all.
•  Do not memorise these word for word. Memorised answers sound memorised, and interviewers notice immediately.
•  Learn the shape and the key facts. Then say it in your own words, differently each time.
•  The answers are written in short sentences on purpose. They are meant to be spoken by someone still building confidence in English. Short sentences are also simply better in interviews - long sentences are where people get lost.
•  Say every answer aloud at least five times before you use it. Record yourself once.
Know the facts, the order, and the point. Never the sentences.
<a id="s14-section-a-self-introduction"></a>

### Section A - Self introduction
Asked first, almost always. It sets the tone for everything after. Target: 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Not 5 minutes.

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Now | Who you are and what you do today. One or two sentences |
| Journey | How you got here. Brief. Companies and what changed at each |
| Strongest work | One concrete achievement with a number |
| What you are good at | Two or three technical strengths |
| Why here | One sentence on why you are in this conversation |

<a id="s14-a1-the-standard-answer"></a>

#### A1. The standard answer
I am a backend engineer with about five years of experience, mostly in C# and .NET. Right now I work at Casepoint, where we build a large eDiscovery platform used by US federal agencies. My work is backend - APIs, data processing pipelines, and performance work. Before this I worked at Vision Infotech and Zibma Infotech, where I built enterprise and ERP applications. The work I am most proud of is an email processing pipeline at Casepoint. It used to take seven to ten hours. I redesigned it using AWS SES, EventBridge and SQS, and brought it down to about one to one and a half hours. That is roughly an eighty percent improvement. My core strengths are backend design in .NET, SQL performance, and event-driven processing with queues. I am looking for a role where I can work on larger scale distributed systems, which is why I was interested in this position.
Notes on delivery: pause after the number. Let it land. Do not rush into the next sentence. The 80% figure is the single most memorable thing you will say in the first minute.
<a id="s14-a2-the-technical-round-version-45-seconds"></a>

#### A2. The technical-round version - 45 seconds
Shorter, more technical. Skip the career history, go straight to what you build.
I am a .NET backend engineer, about five years. I work on an eDiscovery platform at Casepoint - backend services, data processing, and performance. Most of my recent work is around asynchronous processing with queues, SQL optimisation, and refactoring legacy modules. Outside work I built a distributed job processing platform to go deeper on queues, retries, idempotency and worker scaling.
<a id="s14-a3-the-overseas-role-version-use-this-for-every-application-"></a>

#### A3. The overseas-role version - use this for every application outside India
One change and one addition. The change: lead with the domain, because eDiscovery and government-records work is far rarer than .NET experience and it is what makes you memorable. The addition: name the visa situation yourself, in a neutral sentence, so it is not an awkward discovery later.
I am a backend engineer with about five years in C# and .NET. For the last year and a half I have worked on a large-scale eDiscovery platform used by US federal agencies - so document processing at volume, FOIA workflows, and multilingual ingestion, where correctness matters more than speed of delivery. The work I am proudest of is redesigning an email processing pipeline from a sequential job taking seven to ten hours into an event-driven one using SES, EventBridge and SQS, which brought it down to about an hour and a half. I am based in India and I would need sponsorship, and I am specifically looking to move rather than exploring - I have already started the paperwork on my side.
That last sentence does real work. It signals seriousness rather than curiosity, and recruiters abroad are wary of candidates who apply from overseas and then decline because they were never actually going to move.
<a id="s14-a4-walk-me-through-your-resume"></a>

#### A4. "Walk me through your resume"
A different question from "tell me about yourself" - they want chronology and reasons for each move. Keep it to two minutes and give each move a reason, not just a date.
I started at Zibma in Surat in late 2021, building ERP applications for educational institutions. That is where I learned to work with large datasets - we handled well over a hundred thousand records and I did a lot of query optimisation. After about two years I moved to Vision Infotech because I wanted broader backend work across different clients rather than one product. Then in May 2025 I joined Casepoint, and that was the biggest step - a genuinely large-scale platform, US federal agency customers, and the first time I worked on something where a mistake has real consequences. That is also where the email pipeline work happened. Alongside that, over the last several months I have been deliberately filling in my fundamentals - algorithms, system design, distributed systems - and I built a distributed job processing platform to apply them.
<a id="s14-section-b-the-hard-career-questions"></a>

### Section B - The hard career questions
These are the questions where candidates lose offers, and they are entirely answerable. B1 to B8 come up everywhere. B9 onwards come up when you are applying from India to a role abroad, and almost nobody prepares them.
<a id="s14-b1-why-do-you-want-to-leave-your-current-company"></a>

#### B1. "Why do you want to leave your current company?"
Asked every single time. Never mention salary, your manager, or frustration. Always move toward something.
I have had good ownership at Casepoint. I work on a platform that is genuinely large scale and used by federal agencies, so correctness matters a lot. What I want now is to go deeper into distributed systems at a bigger scale, and to work somewhere with a longer technical runway. That is the main reason. I am not unhappy where I am. I just think I am ready for a bigger problem.
<a id="s14-b2-you-have-almost-five-years-of-experience-why-is-your-curr"></a>

#### B2. "You have almost five years of experience. Why is your current salary low?"
Direct question, asked often in India. Answer it calmly and without embarrassment. Do not apologise.
I started my career in Surat, at smaller companies, and my salary reflects where I started rather than what I do now. I did not move for money in those years. I stayed because I was getting real ownership and learning a lot. Now I know my work is worth more than what I am paid, so I am looking at the market rate for my level rather than a percentage on top of my current number.
This is the most important answer in the document. It converts your weakest fact into a sign of maturity. Practise it until it is completely comfortable, because any discomfort in your voice tells them the number is negotiable downward.
<a id="s14-b3-why-did-you-not-try-for-big-companies-in-these-four-or-fi"></a>

#### B3. "Why did you not try for big companies in these four or five years? Why now?"
The sharpest version of the question. Be honest. Honesty is genuinely the strongest answer here.
Honestly, for a long time I did not know what the bar actually was. I came from a smaller city and a BCA background, and nobody around me was interviewing at those companies. I focused on the work in front of me and I got good at building and delivering. What changed is Casepoint. Working on a platform at that scale showed me what large systems actually look like, and it also showed me the gaps in my own knowledge. I could use things like queues and async processing, but I could not always explain why they worked. So for the last several months I have been fixing that properly - data structures and algorithms, system design, distributed systems fundamentals - and I built a distributed job processing platform to apply it. I am aiming higher now because I have actually done the work to be ready, not just because I want a bigger salary.
Why this works: it is true, it is specific, it explains the change with a real trigger, and it ends on evidence rather than ambition. Interviewers respect a candidate who names their own gaps.
<a id="s14-b4-you-have-changed-three-companies-in-about-five-years-are-"></a>

#### B4. "You have changed three companies in about five years. Are you stable?"
I was at Zibma for over two years building ERP systems. I moved to Vision Infotech for broader backend work with different clients. I moved to Casepoint because I wanted to work on a large-scale product rather than project work, and that is what I have been doing since. I am not looking to move often. I am looking for the right place to stay for a long time, which is part of why I am asking questions about the team and the technical roadmap.
<a id="s14-b5-why-do-you-want-to-join-our-company"></a>

#### B5. "Why do you want to join our company?"
Never generic. Research the company for ten minutes before every call. Name something specific.
Two reasons. First, the technical fit - you work heavily in .NET, and that is where my depth is, so I would be productive quickly rather than spending six months learning a new stack. Second, the scale and the domain. [Name something specific about their product or engineering.] I also looked at how your engineering team is structured across offices, and that kind of global setup is something I want to be part of long term.
<a id="s14-b6-why-should-we-hire-you"></a>

#### B6. "Why should we hire you?"
Three things. I have five years of real production backend experience in .NET, not just theory. I have delivered measurable results - the clearest one is cutting a pipeline from seven to ten hours down to about one and a half. And I have worked in a high-correctness domain, building for federal agencies, where mistakes are expensive. So I am used to caring about reliability, not just shipping features.
<a id="s14-b7-where-do-you-see-yourself-in-five-years"></a>

#### B7. "Where do you see yourself in five years?"
I want to be a senior backend engineer who owns the design of significant systems, not just the implementation. Technically deep rather than moving to management early. I would also like to be working in an international team by then, which is one of the things that interested me about this company.
<a id="s14-b8-what-is-your-weakness"></a>

#### B8. "What is your weakness?"
Give a real one with a real fix. Never "I work too hard."
For a long time my knowledge was practical but not deep. I could use things correctly without being able to explain the internals. Async and await is a good example - I used it for years before I really understood the state machine underneath. I have been fixing that deliberately over the last several months by studying fundamentals properly and testing myself instead of just reading. It is genuinely better now, but it is the gap I have worked hardest on.
<a id="s14-b9-why-should-we-hire-you-rather-than-a-local-candidate-we-d"></a>

#### B9. "Why should we hire you rather than a local candidate we do not need to sponsor?"
The hardest question in this document, and the one you are most likely to be asked by a European or Australian hiring manager. It is a fair question - sponsorship costs them money and time. Do not get defensive and do not apologise. Answer it as a value question, because that is what it is.
That is a fair question, and I would not expect you to sponsor someone who is merely equivalent to a local candidate. What I would bring that is genuinely hard to find is the combination of deep .NET backend experience and eDiscovery and government-records domain knowledge - I have spent the last year and a half on a platform serving US federal agencies, including FOIA workflows and multilingual document processing. That is a small pool of people anywhere in the world. On top of that, I have a measured record on performance work - the pipeline I cut from seven to ten hours down to about ninety minutes. And practically, I am committed rather than exploratory: I have already started my own paperwork, so I am not going to waste your process.
The shape to remember: acknowledge the cost, then name something scarce, then prove it with a number, then remove their risk. Never argue that you are cheaper. That answer devalues you and it is often illegal for them to act on.
<a id="s14-b10-you-have-a-bca-not-a-computer-science-degree-from-a-top-"></a>

#### B10. "You have a BCA, not a computer science degree from a top university. Does that matter?"
Answer without defensiveness. Do not oversell the degree and do not run it down either.
My degree is a BCA from South Gujarat University, so I did not get the theoretical grounding that a top-tier computer science programme gives you. I know that, and it is exactly why I spent the last several months going back and doing it properly - operating systems, networking, database internals, algorithms, distributed systems - and testing myself rather than just reading. What I did get instead was four and a half years of production experience quite early, so I learned to debug real systems and own real deliverables. I would rather be judged on what I can explain and build than on where I studied, and I am comfortable being tested on either.
For Canada and Australia there is a second, purely administrative angle: your BCA needs an equivalency assessment (WES or ACS). If a recruiter raises the degree in that context they are usually asking about paperwork, not ability - answer the paperwork question.
<a id="s14-b11-are-you-comfortable-working-entirely-in-english"></a>

#### B11. "Are you comfortable working entirely in English?"
Do not overclaim and do not undersell. The fact that you are having the conversation is most of the evidence.
Yes. All my work is in English - documentation, code review, and the platform I work on is for US customers, so English is the working language day to day. My accent takes some people a moment, and if I am ever unclear I would rather you stop me than guess. Written English is my stronger side. I have been deliberately practising speaking about technical topics out loud for the last several months, which is honestly why this conversation is easier than it would have been a year ago.
Why the admission helps. Naming a limitation and showing the work you did about it reads as self-awareness. Claiming to be fluent and then stalling mid-answer reads as the opposite.
<a id="s14-b12-you-have-never-worked-outside-india-how-do-you-know-you-"></a>

#### B12. "You have never worked outside India. How do you know you will adapt?"
They are testing whether you have thought about it, not whether you can promise it.
You are right, I have not lived abroad, so I am not going to claim I know exactly what it will be like. What I can say is that I already work across a big cultural and time-zone gap - my platform serves US federal agencies, so I work with US stakeholders and US working patterns. And I have thought about the practical side rather than romanticising it: I have looked at cost of living, the visa route and the timeline, and I am treating this as a long-term move rather than a couple of years. The part I am most looking forward to is being in the same building as the people making the product decisions rather than at the other end of a call.
<a id="s14-b13-what-do-you-know-about-our-company-and-about-living-here"></a>

#### B13. "What do you know about our company, and about living here?"
Ten minutes of research before every call. Have one specific product fact and one specific practical fact about the country. The second one is what separates a serious relocation candidate from a speculative applicant.
•  Product fact: something from their engineering blog, a release, or the actual stack.
•  Practical fact: the visa route by name, the rough tax picture, or the city you would live in. "I understand this would be a Critical Skills Employment Permit, and that it goes to Stamp 4 after two years" tells a Dublin recruiter more about your seriousness than any enthusiasm.
•  Never say "I have heard it is a beautiful country." It signals you researched nothing.
<a id="s14-b14-how-do-you-use-ai-tools-in-your-work"></a>

#### B14. "How do you use AI tools in your work?"
A live question at almost every company now, and your resume already mentions Copilot and Claude, so expect it. The wrong answers are "I do not use them" and "I use them for everything".
I use Copilot and Claude most days, for three things mainly. First, understanding unfamiliar code - our platform is large and partly legacy, and having something explain a subsystem back to me is a genuine accelerator. Second, tests and boilerplate. Third, documentation. What I do not do is let them make design decisions, and I review everything they produce the way I would review a junior developer's code, because the failure mode is being confident and wrong. One rule I set for myself is that I never use them for algorithm practice, because it produces an illusion of competence and destroys the exact skill I am trying to build.
<a id="s14-b15-what-would-your-current-manager-say-about-you"></a>

#### B15. "What would your current manager say about you?"
Answer as if they might actually ask. Give a strength and a real critique.
I think he would say I am reliable on the hard, unglamorous work - the performance and legacy refactoring nobody volunteers for - and that I dig until I find the actual cause rather than patching a symptom. The criticism he would give is that I have sometimes gone too deep on a problem before checking whether it was the most valuable thing to fix. I have got better at timeboxing an investigation and coming back with options instead of a finished answer.
<a id="s14-b16-tell-me-about-a-time-you-had-to-learn-something-new-quic"></a>

#### B16. "Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly."
Have a real one ready. Vonage video integration in VCDoctor and the AWS event-driven redesign both work. Structure it: what you did not know, how you closed the gap, what shipped.
When I redesigned the email pipeline, I had used SQS before but I had not designed an event-driven flow across SES and EventBridge, and I did not properly understand delivery guarantees. I spent time on the actual documentation rather than tutorials, built a small throwaway version to prove the message flow first, and specifically worked out what would happen on a duplicate delivery before I wrote the real handler. That was the part I would have got wrong if I had just started coding. It shipped and cut the run time by about eighty percent.
<a id="s14-b17-how-do-you-handle-pressure-or-being-on-call"></a>

#### B17. "How do you handle pressure, or being on call?"
I have worked on a platform where a bad deploy affects federal agency customers, so pressure usually means production. What works for me is process rather than heroics: establish what is actually broken and for whom, stop the bleeding before finding the root cause, keep people informed even when I do not have an answer yet, and write it down afterwards so it does not recur. The part I have had to learn is asking for help earlier. Staying quiet while you struggle feels like ownership and it is not.
<a id="s14-b18-what-kind-of-team-and-manager-do-you-work-best-with"></a>

#### B18. "What kind of team and manager do you work best with?"
I do best with a manager who gives me a problem rather than a task, and who is direct with feedback. I would rather be told clearly that something is wrong than find out later. On the team side, I like code review that is genuinely technical - where people disagree about design and say so - because that is where I have learned the most.
<a id="s14-b19-what-are-you-weakest-at-technically-right-now"></a>

#### B19. "What are you weakest at technically, right now?"
Different from B8: this one wants a current, specific gap. Name one, honestly, and name what you are doing about it. Never name something core to the job you are applying for.
Front-end. I can work with HTML, CSS and Bootstrap enough to be useful, but I would not call myself a front-end engineer and I would not want to claim otherwise. In backend terms, the area I am still actively building is large-scale stream processing - I understand queues well from production, but event-time processing, windowing and Kafka semantics are things I have studied more recently than I have shipped.
<a id="s14-b20-where-else-are-you-interviewing"></a>

#### B20. "Where else are you interviewing?"
Be honest but non-specific. Never name companies unless you have a reason to.
I am in process with a few other companies - mostly .NET-heavy product companies, and a couple of them abroad. I would rather not name them, but I am happy to be transparent about timelines if that helps you plan.
<a id="s14-b21-what-is-your-biggest-professional-achievement"></a>

#### B21. "What is your biggest professional achievement?"
This is the flagship story in 90 seconds, not 5 minutes. Lead with the number.
Taking a pipeline that took seven to ten hours down to about ninety minutes - roughly eighty percent. What makes me proud of it is not the number, it is that the first thing I did was measure where the time actually went instead of assuming. Most of it was sequential processing and blocking waits, not the thing everyone thought it was. Once that was clear the redesign was straightforward - make it event-driven with SES, EventBridge and SQS so work could be processed in parallel by multiple consumers.
<a id="s14-b22-you-have-five-years-of-experience-but-this-is-a-mid-leve"></a>

#### B22. "You have five years of experience but this is a mid-level role. Is that a problem?"
No, and be careful not to sound like you are settling.
I care more about the scope than the label. My experience is broad and production-heavy, but I am honest that my formal fundamentals are newer than my delivery experience, and working in a stronger engineering environment is worth more to me right now than a title. What I would want is a clear conversation about what the next level looks like and roughly when it is reasonable to expect it - and I would rather have that conversation now than in a year.
<a id="s14-section-c-explaining-your-projects"></a>

### Section C - Explaining your projects
You need three versions of every project. Interviewers interrupt, so start short and expand only when asked.
<a id="s14-c1-your-current-work-at-casepoint-the-30-second-version"></a>

#### C1. Your current work at Casepoint - the 30-second version
I work on an eDiscovery platform used by US federal agencies. It handles very large volumes of legal documents and email data. My work is backend - APIs, data processing pipelines, SQL performance, and modernising legacy modules. I also work on FOIA-related features and multilingual content processing.
<a id="s14-c2-the-2-minute-version-add-the-achievement"></a>

#### C3. When they dig deeper - be ready for these

| Question | Have ready |

<a id="s14-c4-your-personal-project-the-distributed-job-platform"></a>

#### C4. Your personal project - the distributed job platform
The one-sentence pitch. It is a distributed job processing platform in .NET. You submit a job over an API, it is stored in Postgres and queued, and multiple workers process it. The interesting part is that it keeps working correctly when things fail - worker crashes, duplicate messages, downstream failures.
The 2-minute version. I built it because I wanted to properly understand the problems behind the tools I had been using at work. The API accepts jobs and stores them in Postgres, then publishes to a queue. Multiple worker processes consume and execute them. The parts I focused on are the failure cases. If a worker dies in the middle of a job, a heartbeat and a reaper process detect it and re-queue the job. Because the queue is at-least-once, the same message can arrive twice, so each job has an idempotency key and a dedup table to make sure it only executes once. Failed jobs retry with exponential backoff and jitter, and after a limit they go to a dead letter queue that I can inspect and replay. I load tested it with k6 and measured the throughput at one worker versus four, so I can show it actually scales horizontally rather than just claiming it.
Questions to expect:
•  Why a queue instead of processing directly? What did that cost you?
•  Walk me through exactly what happens when a worker dies mid-job.
•  How do you get exactly-once processing on an at-least-once queue?
•  Why Postgres? What would change if jobs were much larger?
•  Where is your bottleneck? How do you know? Have the numbers.
•  What would you change if you had to run this in two regions?
•  What is the worst failure mode you did not handle, and why did you decide not to?
That last question is the one that most separates a candidate who built something from a candidate who followed a tutorial. Have a real answer - a scope decision you made deliberately, and the reasoning.

<a id="s14-section-d-the-technical-screening-quickfire"></a>

### Section D - The technical screening quickfire
Recruiters and first-round screeners ask short technical questions to check you are what your resume says. These are not the deep rounds - Documents 02 to 09 build for those. These are 30-to-60-second spoken answers, and the failure mode is rambling rather than being wrong.
The shape for every one: what it is, why it exists, and one consequence that matters in production. That third part is what makes you sound like an engineer rather than a candidate.
<a id="s14-d1-net-framework-versus-modern-net"></a>

#### D1. ".NET Framework versus modern .NET"
The Framework is Windows-only, ships with the operating system, and a machine has one version of it that everything shares. Modern .NET is cross-platform, versioned side by side, and deployed with the application. It is also substantially faster. The Framework only gets security fixes now, so all new work goes to modern .NET. The practical consequence when you migrate is that anything tied to Windows-only APIs, Web Forms, WCF hosting or app domains needs a replacement rather than a recompile.
<a id="s14-d2-what-actually-happens-when-you-await-something"></a>

#### D2. "What actually happens when you await something?"
The compiler rewrites the method into a state machine. When you hit an await on something that has not finished, the method returns to its caller and the thread goes back to the pool. When the operation completes, a continuation resumes the state machine from where it left off. So async does not make anything faster - it stops a thread sitting idle waiting for I/O. That is what lets a server handle far more concurrent requests with the same number of threads.
<a id="s14-d3-why-does-calling-result-deadlock"></a>

#### D3. "Why does calling .Result deadlock?"
The classic case is a UI app or legacy ASP.NET, where there is a synchronisation context. The continuation needs that one context thread to resume, and you are blocking that exact thread waiting for the result, so neither can proceed. ASP.NET Core has no synchronisation context, so it does not deadlock the same way - but blocking still ties up a thread pool thread, and under load that shows up as latency and thread starvation rather than a hang. Either way the answer is async all the way down.
<a id="s14-d4-value-types-versus-reference-types"></a>

#### D4. "Value types versus reference types"
A value type holds the data itself; a reference type holds a pointer to data on the heap. Local variables of value type usually live on the stack, but a value type that is a field of a class lives inside that object on the heap - so "value type means stack" is not quite true. Three consequences matter: copy semantics, boxing when you treat a value type as an object, and equality, because reference types compare identity unless you override it.
<a id="s14-d5-dependency-injection-lifetimes"></a>

#### D5. "Dependency injection lifetimes"
Singleton is one instance for the application, scoped is one per request, transient is a new one every time it is resolved. The bug everyone hits is capturing a scoped service inside a singleton - typically a DbContext. It then outlives its scope, and you get concurrency errors and stale data. .NET will actually throw on that if scope validation is enabled, which it is by default in development.
<a id="s14-d6-ef-core-tracking-and-what-n-1-is"></a>

#### D6. "EF Core tracking, and what N+1 is"
A tracked query puts every entity into the change tracker so SaveChanges can work out a diff. That is what you want for writes and pure overhead for reads, so read-only queries should use AsNoTracking. N+1 is when you load a list and then touch a navigation property in a loop, so you get one query per row instead of one query. You fix it with Include, or better, by projecting straight to a DTO. The way you find it is by logging the generated SQL - you cannot see it by reading the C#.
<a id="s14-d7-when-does-an-index-not-help"></a>

#### D7. "When does an index not help?"
An index gives the engine an ordered structure so it can seek instead of reading every row. It stops helping in three cases: when the predicate is not sargable, so a function wrapped around the column or a leading wildcard; when the query returns a large fraction of the table, where a scan is genuinely cheaper; and when the index does not cover the query, so every match costs an extra lookup. The way to know is the execution plan, not intuition.
<a id="s14-d8-isolation-levels-what-does-each-one-actually-prevent"></a>

#### D8. "Isolation levels - what does each one actually prevent?"
Read uncommitted allows dirty reads. Read committed stops dirty reads but still allows non-repeatable reads and phantoms. Repeatable read stops non-repeatable reads but still allows phantoms. Serializable stops all three, by taking range locks, at a real concurrency cost. Snapshot isolation gives you a consistent point-in-time view without blocking readers, but you can get write skew. The point is that you are trading anomalies against concurrency, and you choose per workload rather than globally.
<a id="s14-d9-what-is-idempotency-and-why-do-you-care"></a>

#### D9. "What is idempotency and why do you care?"
An idempotent operation has the same effect whether you do it once or five times. GET, PUT and DELETE should be idempotent; POST is not. It matters because clients and queues retry. If a payment POST can be retried you need an idempotency key that you store, so the second request returns the first result instead of charging the customer twice. I implemented exactly that in my job platform, with a dedup table keyed on the idempotency key.
<a id="s14-d10-exactly-once-delivery-is-it-real"></a>

#### D10. "Exactly-once delivery - is it real?"
Exactly-once delivery is not achievable in a distributed system, and almost every queue gives you at-least-once, which means duplicates are normal rather than exceptional. What you can achieve is exactly-once processing, at the consumer: you make the handler idempotent, usually with a dedup record written in the same transaction as the effect. If the effect is not transactional - sending an email, calling a third party - you need an outbox pattern or a compensating action instead.
<a id="s14-d11-caching-strategies-and-what-goes-wrong"></a>

#### D11. "Caching strategies, and what goes wrong"
Cache-aside is the default: check the cache, on a miss load from the database and populate. Write-through keeps them consistent at the cost of write latency; write-behind is fast but can lose data. The hard parts are not the strategies, they are invalidation and stampedes - a hot key expiring under load sends every request to the database at the same moment. You handle that with a short lock, staggered expiry times, or serving stale data while one caller refreshes.
<a id="s14-d12-signalr-versus-polling"></a>

#### D12. "SignalR versus polling"
Polling wastes requests and adds latency of about half the poll interval. SignalR keeps a persistent connection - WebSockets when the client supports it, falling back to server-sent events or long polling. The cost is that you now have connection state, so scaling out needs a backplane like Redis, otherwise a message published on one server never reaches clients connected to another. You also have to think about what happens to messages during a reconnection. I hit both of those building the messaging in VCDoctor.
<a id="s14-d13-sqs-versus-kafka-when-would-you-choose-which"></a>

#### D13. "SQS versus Kafka - when would you choose which?"
SQS is a work queue: a message is consumed and deleted, adding consumers is trivial, and you get a managed dead letter queue. Kafka is a durable partitioned log: consumers track their own offsets, messages stay after being read so you can replay them, and ordering is guaranteed within a partition. I would pick SQS to distribute independent tasks - which is exactly what the email pipeline needed - and Kafka when I need replay, several independent consumer groups reading the same stream, or ordering by key.
<a id="s14-d14-horizontal-versus-vertical-scaling"></a>

#### D14. "Horizontal versus vertical scaling"
Vertical is a bigger machine: simple, no code changes, and it has a hard ceiling and a single point of failure. Horizontal is more machines, which is effectively unbounded but forces you to be stateless, to share cache and session state, and eventually to partition data. My job platform scales horizontally because the workers hold no state and the queue does the distribution - and I measured throughput at one worker against four so I can show it rather than assert it.
<a id="s14-d15-an-endpoint-is-slow-in-production-what-do-you-do"></a>

#### D15. "An endpoint is slow in production. What do you do?"
Measure before guessing. Look at p95 and p99 rather than the average, because the average hides the problem people are complaining about. Then split the latency: is the time in the database, in an external call, or in our own code? Correlation IDs and timings in the logs usually answer that, or a trace if we have one. In practice it is most often a missing index, an N+1, or a synchronous call to something slow. I fix the biggest contributor, measure again, and stop when it is fast enough instead of chasing the last millisecond.
<a id="s14-d16-sketch-a-rate-limiter"></a>

#### D16. "Sketch a rate limiter"
Token bucket. Each key has a bucket that refills at a fixed rate up to a maximum. A request takes a token, or gets a 429 with a Retry-After header. Because there are multiple instances, the bucket lives in Redis, and the check-and-decrement has to be atomic - a Lua script or an INCR-with-expiry - otherwise two instances both hand out the last token. A sliding window log is more accurate but stores a timestamp per request, so it costs much more memory at scale.
<a id="s14-d17-what-should-a-backend-engineer-know-about-the-garbage-co"></a>

#### D17. "What should a backend engineer know about the garbage collector?"
It is generational, on the observation that most objects die young - so gen 0 collections are cheap and frequent, and gen 2 is expensive. Anything over roughly 85 kilobytes goes on the large object heap, which is not compacted by default and can fragment. A web server should use server GC, which keeps per-core heaps. But the thing that actually matters in production is allocation rate: the fix for GC pressure is almost always allocating less - pooling, spans, streaming instead of buffering - rather than tuning the collector.
<a id="s14-d18-what-happens-when-a-browser-requests-a-url"></a>

#### D18. "What happens when a browser requests a URL?"
DNS resolution first, working up the cache chain to an authoritative server, to get an IP. Then a TCP handshake, and a TLS handshake on top of it if it is HTTPS - certificate validation and key exchange, ending with a symmetric session key. Then the HTTP request goes out, typically to a load balancer, which picks a backend. The application handles it, probably hitting a cache and a database. The response comes back, and the browser parses HTML and fires off more requests for assets, often from a CDN. The parts I have actually had to reason about at work are connection reuse and keep-alive, and where the load balancer terminates TLS.
<a id="s14-section-e-salary-questions"></a>

### Section E - Salary questions
<a id="s14-e1-the-rule-you-must-not-break"></a>

#### E1. The rule you must not break
Never inflate your current CTC. Indian companies verify it at offer stage using salary slips, Form 16, bank statements and third-party background checks. A mismatch means the offer is withdrawn, and it can follow you. The downside is severe and the gain is small.
The goal is not to hide the number. The goal is to make it irrelevant.
<a id="s14-e2-what-is-your-current-ctc"></a>

#### E2. "What is your current CTC?"
Be honest, and quote your full CTC - including variable, employer PF, gratuity and any benefits. Many people quote only base and under-report themselves. Check your offer letter.
My current fixed CTC is [full number from your letter]. I should mention that it is well below market for my experience. I started at smaller companies in Surat and my salary has followed that starting point rather than the work I am doing now. So I would rather discuss the range for this role than work from a percentage on my current number.
Say the second half in the same breath. Do not give the number and stop - that leaves them anchored.
<a id="s14-e3-what-is-your-expected-ctc"></a>

#### E3. "What is your expected CTC?"
This is the field and question you fully control. Give a real, researched number. Saying "negotiable" or "as per company standards" wastes your one free anchor.
Based on the market for backend engineers at my experience level, and on the scope of this role, I am targeting [28 to 35] lakhs. I am open to discussing the structure - base, variable, stock. What range is budgeted for this position?
Always end by turning the question back. It is the most useful sentence in any salary conversation.
<a id="s14-e4-we-can-offer-a-thirty-to-forty-percent-hike-on-your-curre"></a>

#### E4. "We can offer a thirty to forty percent hike on your current"
This is the default framing in India and it is a trap for you specifically. Thirty percent on your current number is a poor outcome. Refuse the framing, politely, every time.
I understand that is the usual way to look at it, but I would rather not work from a percentage of my current salary, because my current salary is below market. I am looking at what this role and this level are worth. Could you tell me the band you have for this position?
<a id="s14-e5-why-should-we-pay-you-so-much-more-than-you-earn-now"></a>

#### E5. "Why should we pay you so much more than you earn now?"
Because I would be paid for the role, not for my history. My current salary reflects the size of the companies I started at, not my skill level. If you look at what I am delivering - large-scale backend work, a pipeline optimisation that cut eight hours down to one and a half, work in a high-correctness domain - that is the level I am asking to be paid at. And that is the range other companies are discussing with me as well.
<a id="s14-e6-what-is-your-notice-period"></a>

#### E6. "What is your notice period?"
Know your exact number. If it is long, mention flexibility.
My notice period is [X] days. I can try to negotiate an earlier release if the timing matters, and I am happy to discuss a specific start date.
<a id="s14-e7-do-you-have-other-offers"></a>

#### E7. "Do you have other offers?"
Never invent one. It is checkable and it ends conversations.
If you do: "Yes, I am in final stages with a couple of other companies. I would rather be transparent about the timeline than rush anyone."
If you do not: "I am in process with a few other companies but nothing final yet. I would prefer to be honest about that rather than create artificial pressure."
<a id="s14-e8-what-are-your-salary-expectations-in-euros-pounds-dollars"></a>

#### E8. "What are your salary expectations in euros / pounds / dollars?"
The single most common way Indian candidates lose money on an overseas offer is by mentally converting their Indian salary into the local currency and being thrilled. Never convert. Always research the local band using Part 5 and levels.fyi, and quote in the local currency with a local rationale.
For this level in [city], my understanding is that the range is roughly [local band]. I am targeting the middle of that, and I would want to look at the total package including [pension / superannuation / holiday allowance / relocation] rather than base alone. What is budgeted for the role?
Two things never to say: your Indian salary, and "whatever is standard for the location". The first anchors you catastrophically low; the second wastes your anchor completely.
<a id="s14-e9-the-cost-of-living-here-is-high-are-you-comfortable-with-"></a>

#### E9. "The cost of living here is high - are you comfortable with the number?"
Sometimes a genuine kindness and sometimes a softening-up move. Answer as though it is the former, with evidence that you have done the arithmetic.
I have looked at it properly - rent in [city], tax at that band, and what is left. I am comfortable with the number, and I would rather be realistic now than discover a problem after I move. The one thing I would want to understand is the relocation support, because the first two or three months are the expensive part.
<a id="s14-e10-we-pay-local-market-rates-not-a-premium-for-relocating"></a>

#### E10. "We pay local market rates, not a premium for relocating"
That is fine, and it is what I would expect - I am asking to be paid the same as a local engineer at my level, not more. What is separate from salary is the one-off cost of moving, and that is what I would like to discuss: visa and legal fees, and some help with the first few months.
Separating salary from one-off relocation cost is the key move. Companies resist paying a higher salary and routinely agree to a relocation budget, because one is permanent and the other is not.
<a id="s14-e11-how-much-notice-and-how-soon-could-you-actually-be-here"></a>

#### E11. "How much notice, and how soon could you actually be here?"
Give them a total that includes the visa, and show you know the process. Vagueness here reads as not serious.
My notice period is [X] days. On top of that the visa is the longer part - my understanding is that [route] typically takes [rough timeline] once the paperwork is filed. So realistically I would be looking at [total] from an offer, and I would want to start my side of the paperwork as early as you allow.
<a id="s14-section-f-visa-sponsorship-and-relocation-questions"></a>

### Section F - Visa, sponsorship and relocation questions
<a id="s14-f1-the-trap-do-not-create-a-flight-risk"></a>

#### F1. The trap - do not create a flight risk
When applying to an India-based role, do not lead with "I want to move abroad". The hiring manager is filling an India seat and will hear that you will leave in eighteen months. But do not lie either, because you will want their support for a transfer later.
<a id="s14-f2-do-you-plan-to-relocate-abroad-asked-in-an-india-role"></a>

#### F2. "Do you plan to relocate abroad?" (asked in an India role)
Right now I am focused on this role, and honestly the work itself is what interests me. Longer term I am interested in working in a global team, and one of the things that attracted me to this company is that you have real offices and real movement between them. But that is a few years out. My priority now is joining and doing this job well.
<a id="s14-f3-do-you-require-visa-sponsorship-do-you-have-the-right-to-"></a>

#### F3. "Do you require visa sponsorship?" / "Do you have the right to work here?"
Answer in one clear sentence and then move the conversation forward yourself. Hesitation here creates doubt where there is no need for any.
Yes, I would need sponsorship - I am an Indian citizen based in India. For this role I understand the route would be [name it: a Critical Skills Employment Permit / an EU Blue Card / a Skills in Demand visa / an accredited employer work visa], and I have already looked into the requirements and started my own paperwork. I am happy to walk through the timeline if it is useful.
Naming the visa route is the whole trick. A candidate who says "yes, I need sponsorship" sounds like a problem. A candidate who names the route, knows roughly how long it takes, and has already begun sounds like someone who has removed the work from the recruiter's desk.
<a id="s14-f4-why-do-you-want-to-move-to-our-country-direct-overseas-ro"></a>

#### F4. "Why do you want to move to our country?" (direct overseas roles)
Professional reason first, personal second, both specific.

Professionally, [country] has one of the strongest .NET markets in Europe, and your core engineering is based there rather than being a satellite office - I want to be where the main work happens. Personally, I want to work in an international engineering environment, and I am looking at this as a long-term move rather than a short stint.

<a id="s14-f6-the-internal-transfer-conversation-with-your-own-manager"></a>

#### F6. The internal transfer conversation - with your own manager
STRUCK 29 August 2026 - do not have this conversation. The current employer does not offer overseas transfer or relocation, so there is no transfer to ask about, and raising it marks you as a flight risk for no gain. See Part 0A and Part 0B. The script below is left in place because it becomes useful again at a Part 3M employer that genuinely does move people - use it there, not here.
This is the highest-value and most delicate conversation in this document, and it applies to you right now at Casepoint (see Part 2). Done well it opens a route no job board can offer. Done badly it labels you a flight risk and quietly costs you the next promotion.
Three rules before you open your mouth.
•  Do the quiet homework first. Confirm the corporate relationship between the India and US entities, and find out whether anyone has moved before - ask HR as curiosity about the company, not as a request about yourself.
•  Never frame it as leaving. Frame it as wanting more of the same work, closer to it.

If they are non-committal: do not push, and do not repeat the ask for at least two quarters. Instead, keep a written record of the US-facing work you deliver, because an L-1B petition has to argue specialised knowledge and that argument is made of specifics - which modules, which customers, which processes only you understand. The honest risk. In a small number of companies, saying this marks you as leaving. You know your manager and I do not. If the relationship is not good, the safer version is to ask HR about the mobility policy in general terms and say nothing to your manager until you have an actual opening to point at.
<a id="s14-f7-when-could-you-start"></a>

#### F7. "When could you start?"
Never say "immediately" for an overseas role - it tells them you have not understood the process. Give notice period plus visa time, and say you will start your side early.

#### F8. "Will your family relocate with you?"
A logistics question, usually asked because dependant visas are extra paperwork. Answer factually and briefly. Do not volunteer detail about family circumstances, and note that in most jurisdictions there are limits on what they may ask - so keep it short and neutral either way.
Yes / no - and if yes: I would want to understand the dependant visa process and the timeline, and whether that is something the company's immigration support covers.
<a id="s14-f9-have-you-travelled-abroad-before-have-you-ever-been-refus"></a>

#### F9. "Have you travelled abroad before? Have you ever been refused a visa?"
The first is small talk with a purpose - a clean travel history makes a visa officer's decision easier. If you have no travel history, do not apologise for it; nobody is refused a work visa for having an empty passport. The second question is a compliance question and the only correct answer is the truthful one - a prior refusal that you disclose is a manageable fact, and one that you conceal and they discover is fatal.
<a id="s14-f10-adjusted-for-cost-of-living-you-might-be-better-off-in-i"></a>

#### F10. "Adjusted for cost of living you might be better off in India. Why move?"
A genuinely good question, and a test of whether you have thought past the headline number.

I am moving for the kind of engineering work that is concentrated where you are, for the chance to build a career in a market this deep, and honestly for the long-term stability of having residency somewhere rather than a series of assignments. The salary matters, but it is not the reason.

Careful. Do not lie, and do not make PR sound like the point of the job.

### Section G - Negotiation scripts
<a id="s14-g1-when-you-receive-the-offer-never-accept-immediately"></a>

#### G1. When you receive the offer - never accept immediately

<a id="s14-g2-asking-for-more"></a>

<a id="s14-g3-negotiating-level-not-just-money"></a>

#### G3. Negotiating level, not just money
Could I ask how the level was decided? With five years of backend experience and ownership of a large-scale system, I would have expected to be considered at the senior band. I would like to understand the criteria, because the level matters to me for the longer-term path.
Level is worth more than money over a career, and it usually determines transfer eligibility too. Ask about it before you argue about salary.
<a id="s14-g4-getting-relocation-in-writing-for-an-india-role"></a>

#### G4. Getting relocation in writing (for an India role)
One thing that is important to me is the possibility of moving to an overseas office in a few years. Could you confirm in email how internal mobility works - the eligibility period and the process? I am not asking for a commitment, just to understand it properly.
"We support mobility" said on a phone call is worth nothing in eighteen months when your manager has changed. Get it in email.
<a id="s14-g5-if-they-will-not-move-at-all"></a>

#### G5. If they will not move at all
I understand. I would still like to join, so let me ask differently - is there room on the joining bonus, or could we agree a compensation review at six months against clear goals? If neither is possible I understand, and I will confirm my decision by [date].
<a id="s14-g6-the-relocation-package-what-to-actually-ask-for"></a>

#### G6. The relocation package - what to actually ask for
For an overseas offer, this is where the real negotiation is, and most candidates do not know these are negotiable. Ask for the list, not a number - it is much harder to refuse a specific item than a vague request for more.

| Item | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Visa and legal fees | For you and for dependants. Usually paid as standard; confirm it covers the whole family, not just the primary applicant |
| Immigration lawyer | Whether you get a named adviser or are left to file alone. This matters more than the money |
| Flights | One-way for everyone moving, and whether it includes excess baggage |
| Temporary accommodation | The first four to eight weeks. This is the single most useful item, because finding permanent housing from abroad is nearly impossible |
| Relocation lump sum | A cash amount for shipping, deposits and setup. Ask for a figure; the answer is often surprisingly flexible |
| Housing assistance | A deposit guarantee or agent support. In Amsterdam and Dublin this is worth more than the cash |
| Tax advice | One session with an adviser in your first year. Cheap for them, genuinely valuable to you - especially in the Netherlands with the expat ruling |
| Spouse support | Whether the dependant visa carries work rights, and any job-search help |
| Language classes | Germany, Netherlands, Nordics. Also relevant because German language level directly affects your settlement timeline |
| Clawback terms | Read this clause. Most relocation support is repayable if you leave within 12 or 24 months. Know the number before you sign |

<a id="s14-g7-comparing-offers-across-countries"></a>

#### G7. Comparing offers across countries
If you get two offers in different countries, gross salary is close to useless as a comparison. Score them on this instead, and write it down rather than deciding by feel.

| Compare | Why |
|---|---|
| Net monthly income after tax and rent | The only number that describes your actual life. A higher gross with higher tax and higher rent can be a pay cut |
| Years to employer-independent status | Ireland at two years to Stamp 4 is worth a lot of money compared with a route where you are tied to one employer for five |
| Years to permanent residency | The whole point, per Part 2 |
| Dependant work rights | Whether your spouse can work changes household income more than any negotiation will |
| Route out if the job fails | How many days you have to find a new sponsor if you are laid off. This ranges from generous to brutal |
| Whether the office is the engineering centre | A satellite office is a career ceiling regardless of what it pays |

<a id="s14-g8-if-two-processes-are-on-different-timelines"></a>

#### G8. If two processes are on different timelines
Common and awkward: an offer in hand from your second choice while your first choice is at round two. Be honest with both. It works far better than manoeuvring.
To the one you want: "I want to be transparent - I have an offer in hand with a decision date of [date]. I would much rather be in your process, so I wanted to ask whether it is possible to compress the remaining steps."
To the one you have: "Thank you, I am genuinely interested. I have one other process that is close to finishing and I would rather give you a real answer than a rushed one. Could I confirm by [date]?" A reasonable employer will grant a few days. One that refuses any time at all has told you something useful.
<a id="s14-section-h-questions-you-should-ask-them"></a>

### Section H - Questions you should ask them
Always have four or five. Having none signals you are not evaluating them, which weakens you.
<a id="s14-technical"></a>

#### Technical
•  What does the backend stack look like today, and what are you planning to change in the next year?
•  How big is the team, and how is ownership divided?
•  What does the deployment and release process look like?
•  What is the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?
<a id="s14-role-and-growth"></a>

#### Role and growth
•  What would success in this role look like in the first six months?
•  How is technical growth supported here? Is there a senior track separate from management?
•  How are design decisions made - individually, or through review?
<a id="s14-mobility-for-an-india-role-ask-casually-mid-conversation-not"></a>

#### Mobility, for an India role (ask casually, mid-conversation, not as a demand)
•  How is the engineering team distributed across offices?
•  Do engineers move between offices? How does that usually work?
•  How many people have moved from the India office to an overseas team in the last couple of years?
<a id="s14-sponsorship-for-an-overseas-role-ask-early-not-at-offer-stag"></a>

#### Sponsorship, for an overseas role (ask early, not at offer stage)
•  Have you sponsored engineers from outside the country recently, and how did that go?
•  How many people on this team relocated to join it? - A team that is already half migrants is a team whose manager finds this normal.
•  Is this office an engineering centre, or does it support teams elsewhere? - The single most important question about your career ceiling.
•  What does the immigration support look like in practice - is there a legal team, or do I file it myself?
•  What happens to the visa if the role or the team changes?
The "how many people relocated to join this team" question is the one that tells you the truth. Ask it neutrally, as curiosity about the company. If they cannot name anyone, the sponsorship is theoretical.
<a id="s14-section-i-answers-that-cost-people-offers"></a>

### Section I - Answers that cost people offers

| Do not say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "I want to leave because of the salary" | "I want a bigger technical challenge" |
| "My manager is difficult" | Never mention people. Talk about the work |
| "I do not have any questions" | Always have four ready |
| "I can do anything, I am flexible" | Name what you are good at. Vagueness reads as no depth |
| "We built..." for everything | Say "I" for your own decisions. They are hiring you, not your team |
| "I do not know" and stop | "I do not know, but here is how I would find out" |
| Inventing a number or a competing offer | Both are checkable. Both end conversations |
| Long rambling answers | Answer, then stop. Let them ask the next question |
| "I am willing to take less because of the visa" | Never trade salary for sponsorship. Ask for relocation support instead |
| "Any country is fine, I just want to move" | Name the country and the reason. Desperation reads as a flight risk to everywhere |
| Apologising for needing sponsorship | State it once, name the route, move on |
| Converting the offer into rupees out loud | Discuss it in the local currency, always |

<a id="s14-section-j-the-three-rules-of-delivery"></a>

### Section J - The three rules of delivery
•  Answer, then stop. The most common mistake is continuing to talk after you have finished. Silence is the interviewer's job to fill, not yours.
•  Short sentences. Especially in English under pressure. Short sentences are clearer and you are far less likely to lose your thread.
•  Numbers make you memorable. Seven to ten hours down to one and a half. Eighty percent. A hundred thousand records. Four workers versus one. Interviewers forget adjectives and remember numbers.
<a id="s14-section-k-the-dry-run"></a>

### Section K - The dry run
From Month 5 Week 20, once a week: have someone pick six of these at random, or number them and roll dice. Answer cold, recorded, no preparation. The point is not to answer well. The point is to find the three you cannot answer at all, which then go on the Weak List like any other gap.

| Set | Questions |
|---|---|
| Opening | Tell me about yourself. Walk me through your resume. What is your biggest achievement? Why are you looking? |
| The uncomfortable four | Why is your salary low? Why did you not try for big companies earlier? Why should we sponsor you over a local candidate? Does your degree matter? |
| Projects | Explain the email pipeline in 30 seconds. Now in two minutes. What would you do differently? Explain the job platform. What failure did you deliberately not handle? |
| Technical quickfire | Any six from Section D, back to back, 60 seconds each |
| Salary | What is your current CTC? What are you expecting? We can offer 30% more than you earn now. What are your expectations in local currency? |
| Visa | Do you need sponsorship? When could you start? Why this country? Are you planning to apply for residency? Have you worked abroad before? |
| Behavioural | Any three at random from Part 1. Under three minutes each, Action-heavy |
| Closing | Do you have any questions for us? Why should we hire you? Where do you see yourself in five years? |
