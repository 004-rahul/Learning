## ➕ How behavioral preparation works in this plan

Behavioral rounds test what you really did in the past. You cannot learn them like async/await. You **collect** real situations, **write** them as stories, and **rehearse** them. This section is the system. The other added sections at the top of this chapter are its tools:

| Tool | Use it for |
|---|---|
| [The story worksheet](#add-beh-worksheet) | Writing one story, with its follow-up answers |
| [The 16 Leadership Principles and the matrix](#add-beh-lp-matrix) | Checking which principles your stories cover |
| [Story preparation for every resume line](#add-beh-resume-stories) | The follow-up questions each resume line will get, and the evidence to collect |
| [Claim-safety rules](#add-beh-rules) | Keeping every sentence true, and the list of current risky lines |
| [English routine and phrase bank](#add-eng-routine) (end of this chapter) | Saying it clearly |

### The one rule

**Never invent.** Not a story, not a number, not what another person said or felt. If you do not know a number, leave the field empty and write **VERIFY** next to it. An empty field is honest. An invented number fails at the first follow-up question, and then the interviewer doubts everything else you said.

### The three phases

| Weeks | Phase | What you do | When | Output by the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Collect | A 2-minute work journal every working day. A list of candidate situations | 2 minutes daily, plus 15 minutes in the Sunday behavioral block | A journal with one line per working day, and a candidate list |
| 5–20 | Write | One story per week, written with the worksheet and mapped to the 16 Amazon Leadership Principles | The Sunday behavioral block | 10–12 strong, real stories by Week 20. More only if real material exists |
| 20 onward | Rehearse | Random order, under 3 minutes each, recorded. Re-record the weakest 3 every week | The Sunday behavioral block, plus the dry run in chapter 16 | Stories you can tell in any order and defend under "why?" |

Week numbers are plan weeks. If you read this after Week 4 and have no journal yet, start the journal today and do the Weeks 1–4 work in parallel with the first stories.

### Weeks 1–4: collect

**The work journal.** Start it in Week 1, and no later than Week 2. One line per working day. Two minutes. Write facts, not nice sentences.

| Date | What happened (one line) | What I decided or did | What happened next | Numbers to check later |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

- You may write the journal in any language. The story is written in English later.
- Write only what you could say in an interview. Never copy code, customer names, or customer data into your notes.
- Small things count. A code review comment that changed your mind is an "Earn Trust" story. A design discussion where you argued with data is a "Have Backbone" story. You will forget both in a month if you do not write them down the same day.

**The candidate list.** On each Sunday of Weeks 1–4, go through these prompts. For each one, write any real situation you remember, from any of your three jobs. Use your own records to help your memory: your Azure DevOps work items, your pull requests, your sent emails, release notes. Only look at what you are allowed to see, and do not export anything.

| Prompt | Where to look first |
|---|---|
| Something was slow, and I made it faster | The email pipeline, the embedding counts, SQL tuning, the 100k-record work at Zibma |
| A production problem I fixed | Incidents at Vision, root-cause fixes at Zibma |
| I changed code I did not write | Legacy tracing at Casepoint |
| I connected to an external system | Payments, SignalR/FCM, Vonage, Google Maps, Firebase |
| A requirement was unclear | FOIA workflows, multilingual processing, client modules at Vision |
| I made a mistake, or I was wrong | The journal. Code review history |
| I got hard feedback | Code review comments, sprint reviews, one-to-ones |
| I disagreed with someone | Design discussions, estimates, priorities |
| I helped a teammate or explained something | New joiners, code reviews you gave |
| A tight deadline, or two priorities at once | Sprint history |
| I said no, or pushed back on scope | Client or product requests |
| I learned something fast | New AWS services, new integrations |

For each line, mark three things: **Real?** (yes / not sure), **Evidence?** (what record exists), **Numbers known?** (known / range / unknown). Keep the "not sure" lines. Check them later. Do not promote them to stories until they are confirmed.

### Weeks 5–20: write one story per week

Each week, in the Sunday behavioral block:

1. Pick the week's story (order below).
2. Fill in the [worksheet](#add-beh-worksheet) from memory. About 20 minutes.
3. Mark every number: **K** (known, from a record), **R** (a range from memory), or **V** (VERIFY, left empty).
4. During the week, check the V numbers against records where you are allowed to.
5. Write answers to the 8 follow-up questions. Use the question list for that resume line in [Story preparation](#add-beh-resume-stories).
6. Write the 30-second version. Say it aloud twice with a timer.
7. Mark the story in the [LP matrix](#add-beh-lp-matrix).

**A suggested order.** Strongest material first, so the most-probed stories get the most rehearsal time.

| Week | Story | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Email-processing pipeline (flagship) | — |
| 6 | Embedding-module eligibility counts | — |
| 7 | SQL and index tuning (choose the best real example) | — |
| 8 | Hardest production incident at Vision | — |
| 9 | Tracing legacy code before changing it | — |
| 10 | FOIA workflows or multilingual processing | — |
| 11 | 100k+ record datasets at Zibma | — |
| 12 | Payments integration | — |
| 13 | SignalR / FCM real-time features | — |
| 14–17 | Gap stories: a disagreement, a time you were wrong or got hard feedback, helping someone, a deadline or priority conflict | Only if a real example is in the journal. If not, use the week to deepen the follow-up answers of an existing story |
| 18–19 | Rewrite the two weakest stories. Fill the empty rows of the LP matrix | — |
| 20 | The distributed job platform | Only if it is built and measured. If not, start rehearsal instead |

**When is a story "strong"?** Only when all of these are true:

- Your own actions are clear and separate from the team's.
- The result has a measurement method, or you say honestly that it was not measured.
- All 8 follow-up answers are written.
- The 30-second version takes 40 seconds or less when spoken.
- It maps to at least two principles.
- Nothing in it breaks a [claim-safety rule](#add-beh-rules).

**Why 10–12 and not 20.** Chapter 16 Part 1 asks for twenty stories, five per week in Weeks 17–20. This plan replaces that schedule. Each story needs eight prepared follow-up answers, and twelve deep stories beat twenty shallow ones. If your journal gives you more real material, add more stories. Never add stories to reach a number.

### Week 20 onward: rehearse

- **Random order.** Number the stories. Pick with dice, a random number, or ask someone to choose a question from the [30 prompts in section 8](#qs-8). Stories rehearsed in a fixed order turn into a memorised script.
- **Under 3 minutes each.** Use a timer. If you go over, cut Situation, not Action.
- **Record each one.** Listen once. Score it with the [weekly rubric](#add-eng-routine).
- **Re-record the weakest 3 every week.**
- **Practise follow-ups.** Ask a friend, or record yourself answering, "why?" three times in a row on the same story.
- **Never memorise sentences.** Know the facts, the order, the decision and the result. Say it a little differently each time.
- From Week 20, also run the weekly dry run in [chapter 16, Section K](16_CAREER_APPLICATIONS_RELOCATION.md#s14-section-k-the-dry-run).

## ➕ The story worksheet

One worksheet per story. Copy it into the tracker or a notebook. Fill it from memory first. Then check the records. Leave unknowns empty and write VERIFY.

**Number status key:** K = known, from a record. R = a range from memory (say "about"). V = VERIFY (do not say it aloud yet).

### Part 1: the facts

| Field | Your answer | How to fill it |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | | A short handle you will recognise. "Email pipeline", not "My 80% win" |
| **Date and team** | | Month and year. Company, team or area. Your role at the time |
| **Situation** (2 sentences) | | What the system did, and what was wrong. Two sentences. Really two |
| **Task / your responsibility** | | What YOU owned. Who gave it to you, or was it your idea? |
| **Constraints** | | Time, data size, legacy code, production risk, things you could not change |
| **Options considered** | | At least two options. Why you rejected each one |
| **Actions YOU took** (numbered) | 1.<br>2.<br>3.<br>4.<br>5. | One action per line. Start with a verb: measured, traced, proposed, built, tested, released. Write what others did on a separate line marked "team" |
| **Result and how it was measured** | | Before and after. The method (logs, timer, report). Was the workload the same? Mark each number K, R or V |
| **What didn't work** | | A first attempt that failed, a surprise, a mistake |
| **What you learned and changed later** | | A real change in how you work now. Not a slogan |
| **LPs** (3–5) | | From the [matrix](#add-beh-lp-matrix). More than five means the story is not strong evidence for any of them |
| **Evidence you can show** | | What exists: a work item, a pull request, a dashboard, an email, a release note. Describe it. Never copy it out of the company |

### Part 2: eight follow-up questions

Start from the questions for this resume line in [Story preparation](#add-beh-resume-stories). If the story has no resume line, use these eight defaults.

| # | Likely question | Prepared answer (facts only) | Where each fact comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What exactly was your part, and what did the team do? | | |
| 2 | How did you measure before and after? | | |
| 3 | What alternatives did you consider, and why did you reject them? | | |
| 4 | What was the hardest part technically? | | |
| 5 | What went wrong, or surprised you? | | |
| 6 | Who disagreed, and how did you handle it? | | |
| 7 | What would you do differently now? | | |
| 8 | What happened afterwards? Did it hold up? | | |

### Part 3: three versions

| Version | Length | Shape | Your text |
|---|---|---|---|
| **30-second** | 3–4 sentences | "At [company], [system] had a problem: [problem + number]. I [main action]. As a result, [result + number], measured by [method]." | |
| **2-minute** | About 250 words | Situation (2 sentences) → Task (1 sentence) → Action (most of the time: what you checked, what you decided, what you rejected and why) → Result (number + method) → one learning | |
| **Full** | 3–5 minutes with questions | The 2-minute version, plus the options you rejected, what did not work, and what you changed later. Use it when the interviewer says "tell me more" | |

### Before you mark the story "done"

- [ ] Every "I" is true. Every "we" is really shared work.
- [ ] No number is marked V in the spoken versions.
- [ ] All 8 follow-up answers are written.
- [ ] The 30-second version is timed at 40 seconds or less.
- [ ] There is one real learning.
- [ ] It passes the [claim-safety rules](#add-beh-rules).

## ➕ Amazon's 16 Leadership Principles and the story matrix

Official source: [Amazon Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles). Read the official wording once. The table below is a plain-English summary, not the official text.

Amazon has **16** principles. "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" is **one** principle with two halves: you challenge, and then you commit. Some websites split it and count 17. Do not.

| # | Principle | Plain meaning | Typical question shapes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer Obsession | Start from what the customer needs, and work backwards. Earn and keep their trust | "Tell me about a time you went beyond what the customer asked." "…when the customer's need and the deadline conflicted." "…when you changed something because of customer feedback." |
| 2 | Ownership | Think long term. Act for the whole company. Never say "that's not my job" | "…you fixed something outside your area." "…you saw a problem nobody owned." "…you made a decision without your manager." |
| 3 | Invent and Simplify | Look for new ideas, and make things simpler | "…you simplified a complex process or piece of code." "…you found a new way to solve an old problem." |
| 4 | Are Right, A Lot | Good judgment. Look for different views. Try to prove your own beliefs wrong | "…you decided with incomplete data." "…your first judgment was wrong. What did you do?" |
| 5 | Learn and Be Curious | Keep learning. Explore new things | "…you learned something new quickly." "…something you taught yourself recently." |
| 6 | Hire and Develop the Best | Raise the level of the people around you. Coach and help them grow | "…you helped someone grow." "…you onboarded or mentored someone." |
| 7 | Insist on the Highest Standards | Keep a high bar. Fix problems so they stay fixed | "…you refused to accept 'good enough'." "…you improved quality, testing or review." |
| 8 | Think Big | Set a bold direction. See beyond the current ticket | "…you proposed something bigger than you were asked to do." |
| 9 | Bias for Action | Speed matters. Many decisions can be reversed, so take calculated risks | "…you acted without full information." "…you took a calculated risk." |
| 10 | Frugality | Do more with less | "…you delivered with limited time, people or infrastructure." |
| 11 | Earn Trust | Listen, speak honestly, treat people with respect. Be openly self-critical | "…you received difficult feedback." "…you were wrong." "…you admitted a mistake." |
| 12 | Dive Deep | Know the details. Find the root cause. Be suspicious when the numbers and the story disagree | "…the hardest bug you debugged." "…you found the real root cause." "…the data disagreed with what people believed." |
| 13 | Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit | Challenge a decision respectfully, even when it is uncomfortable. Once it is decided, commit fully | "…you disagreed with your manager or a senior engineer." "…you were overruled. What did you do next?" |
| 14 | Deliver Results | Deliver the important results, on time and with quality, despite obstacles | "…a tight deadline." "…an obstacle that almost stopped you." |
| 15 | Strive to be Earth's Best Employer | Make work safer, fairer and better for the people around you. Lead with empathy | "…you made your team's working life better." "…you supported a colleague who was struggling." |
| 16 | Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility | Think about the wider effect of your work on users, society and the future | "…you considered impact beyond your feature (privacy, security, accessibility)." "…you made a decision that was right but not easy." |

**Notes for an SDE-2 candidate.**

- You do not need management stories. For "Hire and Develop the Best", helping a new joiner, a careful code review, or explaining a module counts, but only if it really happened.
- Chapter 16 Part 1 marks seven principles as high-frequency: Customer Obsession, Ownership, Bias for Action, Earn Trust, Dive Deep, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, and Deliver Results. Aim for two strong stories for each of those.
- Have Backbone and Earn Trust are the two most under-prepared. Both need real examples from the journal.
- Try not to use the same story twice in one interview loop. This is why you need 10–12 stories, not 4.

## ➕ Story preparation for every resume line

Every line on your resume is a promise: "I can talk about this for five minutes and answer questions." For each line below you get three things:

- **The story it can become**, as a frame. You fill in the content from your memory and records.
- **The principles** it can support.
- **The follow-up questions** an interviewer will ask, and **the evidence you must collect** for each one.

The right-hand column is **not** an answer. It is a list of things to find. If you cannot find one, write "I don't know / not measured" in your worksheet. That is a valid answer. An invented one is not.

### 1. Email-processing pipeline (Casepoint) — the flagship

**Resume line:** cut an email-processing run from 7–10 hours to about 1–1.5 hours by rebuilding a sequential job as an event-driven pipeline on AWS SES, EventBridge and SQS with concurrent consumers.

**Story frame:** a long sequential job → you found where the time went → you rebuilt it so work runs in parallel → the run became much shorter.
**Principles:** Deliver Results, Dive Deep, Invent and Simplify, Ownership. Insist on the Highest Standards only if you can describe a real correctness check.

This is the story you will be asked about most. Prepare it as a set of answers, not one speech.

| # | Question you will get | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What did the job do, step by step, before you changed it?" | The old flow in 4–6 steps: what started it, what each step did, where the emails came from and where the results went. Draw it once on paper |
| 2 | "Where did the 7–10 hours go? How do you know?" | What you measured (logs, timings, database stats, a profiler) and what it showed. If there was no measurement before the change, say so |
| 3 | "Why did one run take 7 hours and another 10?" | What made runs differ: volume, time of day, retries, other load. If unknown, write "unknown" |
| 4 | "Was the workload the same before and after?" | The volume per run before and after, if known. How the 1–1.5 hours was timed, and over how many runs |
| 5 | "What did SES, EventBridge and SQS each do in your design? What else did you consider?" | The role of each service. Which ones already existed in the account. The alternatives you thought about, and why you rejected them |
| 6 | "How many consumers? How did you choose the concurrency?" | The real number or setting. How it was chosen (a test, a limit of a downstream system, a guess). What stopped you going higher |
| 7 | "SQS can deliver a message more than once. What happened with duplicates?" | Exactly what the code did if the same message arrived twice. If nothing special was done, write that down. Do not claim idempotency handling unless it existed |
| 8 | "What happened when processing a message failed?" | Retry behaviour, visibility timeout, whether a dead-letter queue existed, how failures were noticed. Only what was real |
| 9 | "How did you check that nothing was lost or processed twice?" | The real check: counts before and after, comparison with the old output, a test run, QA sign-off |
| 10 | "What was your part, and what did others do?" | Who asked for the change, who designed it, who reviewed, who tested, who deployed. Was the event-driven idea yours? |

Also prepare if time allows: what became the new slowest step; how cost changed; how long the work took; what you would do differently; whether it still runs this way. Chapter 16 scripts B16, B21, C2 and C3 already make specific claims about this story. Check each one against this evidence before you use it (see [the risky lines](#add-beh-rules)).

### 2. Embedding-module document-eligibility counts (Casepoint)

**Resume line:** document-eligibility counts went from 15–20 minutes over 3M documents (with intermittent failures) to 3–4 minutes over 6M documents, by consolidating eleven near-identical queries into two set-based executions.

**Status:** this line is on every resume and cover letter, but no story or follow-up answers exist yet. Write it in Week 6. It must be ready before the first application.

**Story frame:** eleven near-identical queries → you saw the repetition → you replaced them with two set-based executions → faster, on twice the data.
**Principles:** Invent and Simplify, Dive Deep, Deliver Results. Insist on the Highest Standards if you verified the counts carefully. Frugality only if no extra hardware or service was needed (check).

| # | Question you will get | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What is 'document eligibility', and why does the embedding module need these counts?" | The eligibility rules in plain words. Who or what used the counts (a screen, a job, a report). Why speed mattered to them |
| 2 | "What were the eleven queries?" | A name for each count and what it filtered on. Where each one lived (stored procedure, ORM code, inline SQL). How they ran: one after another, in one connection, from one service? |
| 3 | "You say 'near-identical'. What was the same, and what was different?" | The shared part (tables, joins, base filter) and the part that changed per count |
| 4 | "How did two set-based queries return eleven counts?" | The technique you actually used, written in your words. Why two executions and not one, and what each one returned |
| 5 | "Why did it fail intermittently before?" | The real error (timeout, deadlock, memory, connection) and how often it happened. How you knew the cause. If it was never proven, write "my best understanding was…" |
| 6 | "Did the failures stop? How do you know?" | Since when, and what showed it (job history, monitoring, no tickets). If nobody tracked it, do not claim it |
| 7 | "How did you verify that the new counts were identical to the old ones?" | The comparison: the same data through both versions, all eleven numbers compared, on how many cases. Any difference you found, and how you fixed it |
| 8 | "How was 3M → 6M measured? Were the timings on comparable data and hardware?" | Where each document count came from. How the 15–20 and 3–4 minutes were timed (logs, query statistics, a stopwatch). Whether the environment was the same |
| 9 | "Which database was this on? Did you change indexes too?" | SQL Server, PostgreSQL, or both. Any index change. The execution plan before and after, if you looked |
| 10 | "What was your part?" | Who found the problem, who proposed consolidating, who reviewed, who tested, who released |

### 3. SQL and index tuning — SQL Server and PostgreSQL (Casepoint); SQL, stored procedures, indexes and pagination (Vision)

**Story frame:** one slow query → you read the execution plan → you made one or two precise changes → measured improvement. Pick your single best real example from each employer. Two small true stories are better than one big vague one.
**Principles:** Dive Deep, Deliver Results, Insist on the Highest Standards, Frugality.

| # | Question you will get | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Pick one slow query. What did it do, and how slow was it?" | One specific query or procedure. The before time (or a range). How users or the team noticed |
| 2 | "How did you find the cause?" | The tool: actual execution plan in SSMS, `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` in PostgreSQL, `SET STATISTICS IO/TIME`, logs. What the plan showed (scan, key lookup, sort, bad row estimate) |
| 3 | "What exactly did you change?" | The index (columns, order, included columns) or the rewrite (for example, removing a function on a column), or the procedure change |
| 4 | "Why that column order in the index?" | The WHERE and ORDER BY columns, and the reasoning you used at the time |
| 5 | "What did the new index cost on writes and storage?" | Whether you checked insert/update impact and index size. If not, write what you would check now |
| 6 | "How did you do pagination, and why?" (Vision) | OFFSET/FETCH or keyset, the page size, how the total count was computed, and what problem paging solved |
| 7 | "What was different between SQL Server and PostgreSQL in your work?" | Only real differences you met (syntax, plan reading, index types). If you mostly used one, say which |
| 8 | "How did you test the change before production?" | Test data volume, result comparison, review |
| 9 | "Did it stay fast later?" | Any regression later (for example, parameter sniffing or stale statistics). Only if it really happened |

### 4. FOIA workflows and multilingual processing (Casepoint)

**Story frame:** a workflow where correctness and the user's rules matter, or handling text in many languages without breaking it.
**Principles:** Customer Obsession, Insist on the Highest Standards, Learn and Be Curious. Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility only if there was a real privacy or data-protection decision.

Outside the US, do not lead with FOIA (see the [chapter 16 consistency notes](16_CAREER_APPLICATIONS_RELOCATION.md#add-career-consistency)). Still prepare it, because the line is on your resume.

| # | Question you will get | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What is a FOIA workflow, in simple words, and what part did you build?" | A one-sentence description of the user's process. The feature or module you worked on. Your exact part |
| 2 | "Who were the users, and what did they need?" | The type of user and one real requirement you implemented. Where it came from (specification, product owner, bug report) |
| 3 | "What made it hard?" | One rule, status flow, deadline field, permission or edge case you met |
| 4 | "How did you make sure the result was correct?" | Tests, review, QA, or a check you added |
| 5 | "What does 'multilingual processing' mean in your work?" | What processing it was, exactly. Only the part you did |
| 6 | "What problems did different languages cause?" | Encoding, Unicode, right-to-left text, collation, search — only the ones you really met |
| 7 | "How did you test languages you cannot read?" | Test files, sample data, the comparison method |
| 8 | "What would you improve now?" | One concrete improvement |

### 5. Tracing legacy code paths before changing them (Casepoint)

**Story frame:** a risky change in old code → you traced where the behaviour really came from → you protected existing behaviour → a safe release.
**Principles:** Dive Deep, Ownership, Insist on the Highest Standards. Earn Trust if you raised a risk to others.

| # | Question you will get | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Give me one example. What did you need to change, and why was it risky?" | The module, what it did, and what could break |
| 2 | "How did you trace the code path?" | Your real method: debugger, logs, call-hierarchy search, reading stored procedures, a SQL trace, asking a senior colleague |
| 3 | "What did you find that surprised you?" | A hidden caller, a side effect, duplicate logic, a configuration switch |
| 4 | "How did you protect the existing behaviour?" | Tests written before the change, comparison with the old output, a flag. Only what was real |
| 5 | "How did you share what you found?" | A wiki page, the work item, the PR description, a talk with the team |
| 6 | "Did anything break after release?" | The honest answer, and what you learned |
| 7 | "How long did tracing take compared with the change itself?" | A rough split |
| 8 | "When would you rewrite instead of changing it?" | Your own reasoning. An opinion is fine here |

### 6. eDiscovery backend modules, Scrum on Azure DevOps, xUnit and code review (Casepoint)

**Story frame:** owning a backend feature from work item to release, or raising the quality bar with tests and review.
**Principles:** Ownership, Deliver Results, Insist on the Highest Standards. Earn Trust for feedback you received. Hire and Develop the Best only if you really helped others through review.

## ➕ Claim-safety rules

### The rules

1. **Every line must survive one follow-up.** For each sentence, ask yourself "How do you know?" and "What exactly did you do?" If you cannot answer both, cut the sentence or make it smaller.
2. **"I" for what you did. "We" for what the team did.** Never use "we" to hide. Never use "I" to take credit. In the worksheet, mark each action I or team.
3. **Never borrow production claims from study projects.** Dead-letter queues, the outbox pattern, idempotency keys, backoff with jitter, heartbeats and load tests belong to the personal project. Do not say them about Casepoint, Vision or Zibma work unless they really existed there. If you are asked "Did you handle duplicates?" and you did not, say: "Not explicitly. Looking back, I would add a duplicate check. Here is how."
4. **Never quote an unverified number.** A number comes from a record (best) or a memory range (fine, say "about"). A guess is never said aloud.
5. **Say "approximately" honestly:**
   - Use the range you remember: "seven to ten hours", not "eight hours".
   - "About", "roughly" and "in the region of" are only for numbers close to the real one.
   - Say where the number came from: "from the job logs", "from my own timing".
   - Do not turn a range into one precise-sounding number, such as "85.7 per cent".
   - Do not say a percentage you cannot explain. For the email pipeline, the hours are safer than a percentage. If you use one, "roughly 80 per cent or more" is the safe end of the range: 7 → 1.5 hours is about 79 per cent, and 10 → 1 hour is 90 per cent.
   - For the embedding counts, always say both times with both document counts. Do not shorten it to "five times faster".
   - If you do not know: "I don't have the exact figure. It was about X. I would confirm it from Y."
6. **Years of experience.** The resume says "4.5+ years". Counting from December 2021, five years is reached in December 2026. Until then, say "almost five years". Change the resume to "5+" only when it is true, and together with the project, as the plan already says.
7. **Visa and paperwork.** Never name a visa route in a resume or cover letter. Never say the paperwork has started unless it has.
8. **Other people's words.** "My manager would say…" only if it is based on feedback you really received.

## PART D - Behavioural structure

<a id="m5-q18"></a>

#### Q18. Why does Action get 55% of a STAR answer?
Say this. Because Action is what is scored. Situation and Task are context the interviewer needs to understand the Action, and Result is the evidence it worked. What they are assessing is what you decided, what you rejected, and why - and all of that lives in Action.
Why. The rubric asks "what did this person do and how did they think". Three minutes of company background gives the interviewer nothing to write down.
Watch out. Two sentences of Situation. Genuinely two. Then your ownership in one, then the bulk on Action, then a number.

> ⚠️ **Correction (final version, 19 Oct 2026):** [Nuance] '55%' is a coaching heuristic, not an Amazon rubric number. Say 'most of the answer — roughly half or more — is Action'.

<a id="m5-q19"></a>

#### Q19. Why does saying "we" cost you the answer?
Say this. Because the interviewer is hiring you, not your team. "We decided to redesign the pipeline" is unscoreable - it could mean you led it or you watched. "I proposed the event-driven design, and the team pushed back on cost, so I built a small prototype to show the numbers" is scoreable.
Why. The signal they need is attribution. Without "I", there is nothing to attribute.
Watch out. Use "we" deliberately where the work genuinely was collaborative. The contrast makes the "I" credible rather than boastful - all-"I" reads as taking credit.
<a id="m5-q20"></a>

#### Q20. What makes a Have Backbone story work?
Say this. A real disagreement with someone more senior, argued with data rather than opinion, and then - this is the half people omit - full commitment to whatever was decided, including when you lost. "I disagreed, I made my case with numbers, the decision went the other way, and I implemented it properly and it worked out" is a complete answer.
Why. The principle has two clauses. Candidates prepare the disagreement and forget the commit, which reads as someone who argues and then sulks.
Watch out. This is the most under-prepared principle and you need a genuine example. Start noticing these moments at work now and writing them down the same day - you will not remember them in week 17.
<a id="m5-q21"></a>

#### Q21. What makes an Earn Trust story work?
Say this. A time you were wrong, or received hard feedback, and responded well. Not a disguised success - an actual mistake, what it cost, what you did about it, and what changed in how you work.
Why. The principle is testing self-criticism. A candidate who cannot describe being wrong is either not self-aware or not honest, and interviewers read it as the second.
Watch out. Candidates instinctively avoid stories that make them look bad, which is exactly backwards here. The story that makes you look human is the one that scores.
<a id="m5-q22"></a>

#### Q22. How do you answer "what would you do differently?"
Say this. Give a real answer with a reason, and make it a design decision rather than a personal failing. "I would have added the dead letter queue in the first iteration instead of the third - we spent two weeks debugging failures that a DLQ would have made visible in an hour."
Why. It is the maturity signal. Every real project has something you would change, and a candidate who says "nothing" has either not reflected or is not being honest.
Watch out. Have one prepared for every story. It is asked constantly and improvising it produces either a non-answer or an unflattering one.
<a id="m5-q23"></a>

#### Q23. The interviewer keeps asking "why" until you run out. What do you do?
Say this. Say so, and say how you would find out. "I do not know the exact figure - what I remember is the order of magnitude, and I would confirm it from the CloudWatch metrics." Reaching the edge of your knowledge is the design of the Bar Raiser round, not a failure.
Why. They are calibrating depth, and they cannot do that without finding the edge. Inventing a number is the one response that reliably fails, because it is often detectable and it destroys trust in everything you said before.
Watch out. "I do not know, but here is how I would find out" scores well. "I do not know" and silence does not. The second half is the whole answer.
<a id="m5-q24"></a>

#### Q24. How do you quantify a story when you do not have exact numbers?
Say this. Use the order of magnitude and say that is what you are doing. "It was in the region of a hundred thousand records - I do not have the exact figure to hand." That is honest, memorable, and far better than a precise-sounding invention.
Why. Interviewers forget adjectives and remember numbers. "Significantly faster" is forgotten in a minute; "seven to ten hours down to about ninety minutes" is remembered after the loop.
Watch out. Your strongest numbers: 80% reduction, seven-to-ten hours to ninety minutes, 100,000+ records, one worker versus four. Know them cold and pause after saying them.
<a id="m5-q25"></a>

#### Q25. Which of your stories is the flagship, and what does that mean for preparation?
Say this. The email pipeline. It means it will be probed hardest and asked most often, so it needs the deepest preparation: what was actually slow and how you measured it, why SQS rather than an alternative, how you handled duplicate delivery, what surprised you, what you would do differently, and what the new bottleneck became.
Why. A weak follow-up on your best story is worse than not telling it, because it suggests the headline number was not really yours.
Watch out. Prepare it as a set of eight answers, not one story. Then it survives any order of questioning.

## ➕ The daily English routine

The goal is clear, simple, correct technical English. It is not a native accent. You practise English **through** the topics you already study, so it costs almost no extra time.

### Where it fits in the 2-hour plan

| When | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Every study day, at the end of the session | **Explain aloud.** Close your notes. Explain today's topic aloud using the [explanation template](#add-eng-explain). First the 30-second version, then the 2-minute version | 5 minutes |
| Once a week, in the Sunday behavioral block | **Record.** Record one 2-minute explanation (a technical topic or a story). Listen back once. Score it with the rubric below | 15 minutes |
| From Week 20, in the Sunday behavioral block | **Story recordings** replace some technical recordings (see [the behavioral system](#add-beh-system)) | Same 15 minutes |

### Three versions of everything

| Version | Length | When you use it | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **30-second** | 3–4 sentences | Screening calls, "briefly, what is X?" | What it is, why it exists, one consequence |
| **2-minute** | About 250 words | "Explain X to me" | The full [template](#add-eng-explain): definition, why, how, example, trade-off, when not to use |
| **Interview version** | 2 minutes, then questions | Technical rounds | The 2-minute version, then stop. Let them ask. Answer the follow-up in 2–3 sentences |

### How to listen to your recording

Listen for **clarity, not accent**. Your accent is fine if the listener understands every sentence on the first try. Ask yourself only these questions:

- Could someone who does not know me follow every sentence?
- Did I say the main point in the first sentence?
- Did I use the correct technical words?
- Where did I stop, repeat or say "basically"?

### Weekly self-check rubric

Score each line from 1 to 3 after each weekly recording. Write the total in the tracker. The aim is to move each line to 3, one line at a time. Do not try to fix everything in one week.

| Criterion | 1 = needs work | 2 = acceptable | 3 = good |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clarity** | The listener would be lost in places | Mostly clear, one or two confusing sentences | Every sentence is clear the first time |
| **Structure** | Jumps between points | Has an order, but the main point comes late | Main point first, then the template in order, then stop |
| **Correct terms** | Wrong or vague words ("thing", "stuff", "it does the process") | Mostly correct terms, a few vague ones | Exact terms: "thread pool", "index seek", "at-least-once delivery" |
| **Short sentences** | Long sentences joined with "and… and… so…" | Mixed | Most sentences under 15 words |
| **Filler words** | More than 10 per 2 minutes ("basically", "actually", "like", "you know", "so") | 4–10 | 3 or fewer. Silent pauses instead |

**Weekly log (copy into the tracker):**

| Week | Topic recorded | Clarity | Structure | Terms | Short sentences | Fillers | Total /15 | One thing to fix next week |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | | |

**Once a month,** listen to your first recording and your newest one, one after the other. The difference is your real progress.

## ➕ A template for explaining any technical concept

Use the same six steps for every concept. The order does the thinking for you. When you are nervous, you only need to remember the next step.

| Step | Question it answers | Starter sentence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Definition** | What is it, in one sentence? | "[X] is a…" |
| 2. **Why it exists** | What problem does it solve? | "It exists because…" / "Without it, …" |
| 3. **How it works** | What happens inside? | "Inside, it works like this. First… Then…" |
| 4. **Example** | Where would I see it? | "For example, …" |
| 5. **Trade-off** | What does it cost? | "The cost is…" |
| 6. **When not to use it** | When is it the wrong choice? | "I would not use it when…" |

**The 30-second version** uses steps 1, 2 and 5: what it is, why it exists, and one cost or consequence.
**The 2-minute version** uses all six steps, two or three sentences each.

The three examples below are written in simple English on purpose. Read one aloud, close it, and say it again in your own words.

### Example 1: async/await in C#

**30-second version.**
"Async/await lets a method wait for slow input or output, like a database call, without blocking a thread. It exists because threads are limited, and a blocked thread does nothing while it waits. With async/await, the thread goes back to the pool and can serve other requests. It does not make one request faster. It lets a server handle more requests at the same time."

**2-minute version.**
1. **Definition.** "Async/await is a way to write code that waits for I/O, like a database query or an HTTP call, without blocking a thread."
2. **Why it exists.** "A server has a limited number of threads. If a thread blocks while it waits for the database, it does no useful work. Under load, the server runs out of threads, and requests start to queue."
3. **How it works.** "The compiler turns an async method into a state machine. When the code reaches an `await` on a task that is not finished, the method returns an unfinished task to its caller, and the thread is free to do other work. When the I/O completes, the rest of the method continues from where it stopped. In ASP.NET Core, it continues on a thread-pool thread."
4. **Example.** "In a controller, `await _db.Orders.ToListAsync()` frees the thread while SQL Server runs the query."
5. **Trade-off.** "There is a small cost for the state machine. Stack traces are harder to read. And it has to be async all the way: if you block on a task with `.Result` or `.Wait()`, you lose the benefit, and in some older frameworks you can get a deadlock."
6. **When not to use it.** "It does not help CPU-bound work, like a heavy calculation, because the CPU is busy, not waiting. I would also avoid `async void`, except for event handlers, because the caller cannot await it or catch its exceptions."

### Example 2: database index

**30-second version.**
"An index is a separate, sorted structure on one or more columns. It lets the database find rows without reading the whole table. It makes reads much faster for selective queries. The cost is that every insert, update and delete must also update the index, and the index uses extra storage."

**2-minute version.**
1. **Definition.** "An index is a sorted data structure, usually a B-tree, built on one or more columns of a table."
2. **Why it exists.** "Without an index, a query with a WHERE condition must read every row. That is a table scan, and it gets slower as the table grows."
3. **How it works.** "The B-tree keeps the keys sorted. To find a value, the database goes from the root page to a leaf page in a few steps. That is an index seek. In SQL Server, the clustered index stores the table rows themselves in key order. A non-clustered index stores the key and a pointer to the row. If the query needs columns that are not in the index, the database must do an extra lookup for each row. A covering index includes those columns to avoid that."
4. **Example.** "If an `Orders` table has millions of rows and we often run `WHERE CustomerId = @id`, an index on `CustomerId` changes a full scan into a seek over a few pages."
5. **Trade-off.** "Reads get faster, but writes get slower, because every index must be updated on each insert, update and delete. Indexes also use disk and memory. Too many indexes on a busy table can hurt more than they help."
6. **When not to use it.** "It does not help when the query returns a large part of the table, because a scan is then cheaper. It does not help when the condition is not sargable, for example a function around the column or a `LIKE` with a leading wildcard. And a very small table does not need one. The execution plan tells you the truth, not intuition."

### Example 3: idempotency key

**30-second version.**
"An idempotency key is a unique value the client sends with a request, so the server can recognise a retry of the same operation and do it only once. It exists because networks fail, and a client often does not know if its first request succeeded. Without it, a retry can charge a customer twice. The cost is extra storage and a check on every request."

**2-minute version.**
1. **Definition.** "An idempotency key is a unique ID, often a GUID, that the client creates for one logical operation and sends with the request, for example in an `Idempotency-Key` header."
2. **Why it exists.** "A request can time out after the server has already done the work. The client cannot tell the difference, so it retries. Queues also deliver messages at least once, so the same message can arrive twice. For a payment or an order, doing the work twice is a real bug."
3. **How it works.** "The server keeps a table of keys with a unique constraint. When a request arrives, it tries to insert the key. If the insert succeeds, this is the first time: the server does the work and stores the result with the key, ideally in the same transaction. If the key already exists, the server does not do the work again. It returns the stored result. The server should also check that the retry has the same request body, and keys usually expire after some time."
4. **Example.** "A client sends `POST /payments` with a key. The connection drops before the response arrives. The client retries with the same key. The server sees the key, does not charge again, and returns the first result."
5. **Trade-off.** "It needs extra storage and one more write per request. You need an expiry policy. Two identical requests at the same moment need care, which the unique constraint handles. And if the side effect is outside your database, like a call to a payment provider, your own table alone is not enough. You also need the provider's idempotency support, or a pattern like the outbox."
6. **When not to use it.** "Operations that are already idempotent do not need it: a GET, a PUT that sets a value, or a DELETE. It is also unnecessary when a natural unique key already exists, like an order number with a unique constraint."

## ➕ 20 common mistakes in interview English, and the correction

Many of these are normal in Indian English, and people inside India understand them perfectly. In an international interview, the standard form is clearer and avoids a moment of confusion. Fix one or two per week. Do not try to fix all twenty at once.

| # | Instead of | Say | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Please do the needful." | "Could you please [send the logs / review this]?" | Name the action. "The needful" is not understood everywhere |
| 2 | "I will revert back to you." | "I will get back to you." / "I will reply by tomorrow." | Outside India, "revert" means "go back to an earlier state", like reverting a commit. "Revert back" also repeats itself |
| 3 | "Let us discuss about the design." | "Let us discuss the design." / "Let us talk about the design." | "Discuss" takes no "about" |
| 4 | "Can we prepone the meeting?" | "Can we move the meeting earlier?" / "Can we bring it forward?" | "Prepone" is not used outside India |
| 5 | "I have attached the file. Please check the same." | "I have attached the file. Please check it." | Use "it" or "them", not "the same" |
| 6 | "My approach is same like yours." | "My approach is the same as yours." / "My approach is like yours." | "The same as", or "like". Not "same like", and not "same" without "the" |
| 7 | "I created API and stored data in table." | "I created an API and stored the data in a table." | English needs "a", "an" or "the" before most singular nouns. This is the most common error, and the easiest to notice in a recording |
| 8 | "Last year I am working on the pipeline and I see the problem." | "Last year I was working on the pipeline, and I saw the problem." | STAR stories happened in the past. Use past tense all the way through |
| 9 | "I have fixed this bug in 2023." | "I fixed this bug in 2023." | With a specific past time (2023, last week), use the simple past, not "have fixed" |
| 10 | "I am working here since 2025." / "since three years" | "I have been working here since 2025." / "for three years" | "Since" + a point in time; "for" + a length of time. Both need "have been" |
| 11 | "I am having 4.5 years of experience." | "I have 4.5 years of experience." | "Have" for possession is not used in the -ing form |
| 12 | "Myself Rahul." | "I am Rahul." / "My name is Rahul." | "Myself" cannot be the subject of a sentence |
| 13 | "I passed out in 2020." | "I graduated in 2020." | In international English, "passed out" means "fainted" |
| 14 | "I wrote many codes." / "datas", "informations", "feedbacks", "softwares" | "I wrote a lot of code." / "data", "information", "feedback", "software" | These nouns have no plural form. Say "pieces of feedback" or "programs" if you need to count |
| 15 | "I have a doubt." | "I have a question." | In international English, "doubt" means you think something is not true |
| 16 | "It ran less queries." / "very less time" | "It ran fewer queries." / "very little time" | "Fewer" for things you count (queries, rows). "Less" for amounts (time, memory) |
| 17 | "I can able to fix it." | "I can fix it." / "I am able to fix it." | Use one or the other, not both |
| 18 | "Can you explain me the requirement?" | "Can you explain the requirement to me?" | "Explain something to someone" |
| 19 | "Why this query is slow?" | "Why is this query slow?" | In a direct question, the verb comes before the subject. (In an indirect question it does not: "I checked why this query was slow.") |
| 20 | "I will do it today itself." / "It is two years back." | "I will do it today." / "It was two years ago." | "Itself" and "only" for emphasis are not used this way outside India. "Ago", not "back", for past time |

**How to use this list.** In your weekly recording, listen for numbers 7 (articles), 8 (past tense in stories) and 10 (since/for) first. They are the most frequent in technical interviews, and fixing them makes the biggest difference to clarity.
